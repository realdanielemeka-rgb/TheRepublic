    Spruce by Dulux · CAP Plc · 2026

    Show Your True Colours

    The line
    Show Your True Colours.
    The Republic's role
    Digital strategy · Creator and clipper distribution · Pinterest room inspiration · Campaign amplification
    Partners
    Concept and colour identities by 1879 Tech Hub / UACN. Films by KellyStropHQ. Full credits below.
    Launch
    Teasers through March and early April. Hero film published 6 April 2026; character films 7–11 April.

    01 · The problem

    Liking a colour is only the first decision. Before anyone buys paint, they ask whether it will feel like them, whether it will work in their space and which shade to choose. The launch needed digital work that helped people consider the paint, not only notice it.

    02 · The human truth

    People recognise an attitude before they choose a shade.

    03 · The idea

    Put the personalities into conversation, then into rooms people could picture as their own.

    The platform, ten colours as ten recognisable personalities, was created by 1879 Tech Hub / UACN. Our job was the digital route from noticing a colour to choosing one.

    04 · The work

    1. Question. Teasers asked what a colour is allowed to mean, before the range appeared.
    2. Reveal. The hero film and ten character films introduced the cast.
    3. Debate. Live creator pairings put personalities head to head: Steeze White meets Baddie Pink, Old Money Green meets Odogwu Red.
    4. Distribute. A ten-clipper network cut the debates into many edits. Paint became the hook; banter became the edit.
    5. Imagine. AI room inspiration on Pinterest placed the palette in spaces Nigerians could imagine living in, with the profile linking to the Spruce website.
    6. Choose. The route led to the Spruce website, which lets visitors drag to repaint a room, take a six-question quiz and carry the shade into the catalogue.

    The Dispatch · Point of view, films and news

    Journal.

    Essay · Point of view Draft for review

    Daniel Emeka · Managing Director2 min read

    Write for the reply.

    Good brand work gives people a reason to bring their own lives into the story.

    Too much brand content is built to be looked at and left behind. It can be beautifully made. The frame is right, the product is in focus, the caption says something agreeable. Then it reaches somebody’s feed and asks nothing of them. They have seen a version of that scene before. They keep moving.

    When we begin a piece of work, I want us to ask a harder question: what is the person on the other side meant to do with it? Can they recognise something specific from their own life? Can they answer in a way that sounds like them? If the only possible response is to admire the execution, we have probably stopped too early.

    If someone offers you a piece of their story, the least you can do is show that you heard it.

    That changes the way we develop an idea. We look for the detail that makes a broad subject personal. We make room for disagreement, memory and interpretation. We give the audience a clear invitation, then decide what the brand will do with the answer. A question in a caption is easy to add. Building a response into the work takes more thought. The format, the follow-up and the brand’s voice all have to support it.

    With Onga, we asked, “What does home mean to you?” The question left space for people to bring their own memories of food and belonging. Their answers came back into the feed. That return matters. If someone offers you a piece of their story, the least you can do is show that you heard it.

    For Cowbell, the thought was the person behind your first taste. Around Ramadan, the first meal carries work that is often quiet: somebody prepares, remembers, makes sure everyone is ready. Recognising that person gave people a way to speak about care in their own homes. The brand had a role in the conversation because it had first paid attention to the people in it.

    This is how I want us to judge our work before it leaves the room. Who could answer it? What would their answer reveal? Would we know what to do next if they did? If those questions have no convincing answer, we keep working. Creating tomorrow begins with giving somebody a reason to take part today.
